Part 0 · A 10-minute healthcare primer (no prior knowledge assumed)
The world you're building for. In the US, a primary-care clinic (a family doctor's practice) increasingly gets paid
not just per visit but for keeping patients healthy — this is called value-based care (VBC). Insurers score clinics
on quality measures: simple, auditable questions like “what % of your diabetic patients had their blood-sugar
tested this year?” Good scores mean bonus payments; bad scores mean lost revenue.
The core object: a care gap. One patient failing one measure — e.g., “Maria, 62, diabetic, no HbA1c blood test
in 14 months.” Think of it as a lint warning on a patient's record: specific, rule-generated, fixable by a
concrete action (order the test, book the visit).
Your user: the care manager. A nurse/coordinator who works these gaps — calling patients, booking visits. A
5,000-patient panel produces thousands of gaps; they have ~2 hours a day for outreach. Deciding what NOT
to do today is the job.
Glossary for the data: encounter = any visit · observation = a measurement (blood pressure, lab result) ·
condition = a diagnosis · immunization = a vaccine dose · FHIR = the standard JSON format health data ships in
· panel = all patients attributed to the clinic. That's all the domain knowledge required; everything else is
specified below. Never use real patient data anywhere in this assignment.
Part 1 · The situation
A family-practice clinic in Corpus Christi, Texas — ~10,000 patients, newly in value-based contracts — has two
problems at once:
Problem A (the knowledge): the rules of preventive care live in long, unstructured authority documents
(screening guidelines, immunization schedules with footnotes). The clinic follows them from memory. When a
guideline changed recently (colorectal screening moved from age 50 to 45), thousands of patients newly
qualified and nobody recomputed who. “Make the rulebooks work for us — and we must be able to trust it; a
wrong rule applied to 10,000 people is worse than no tool.”
Problem B (the operation): their EHR exports are messy — lab values without units, duplicated encounters,
missing dates, and occasionally an entire data source silently stops arriving for weeks. One care manager has
~2 hours a day for outreach. They were burned by a dashboard tool before: 4,000 unexplained “gaps,” ignored
within a month, and nobody could tell whether outreach work ever resulted in care actually happening.
They ask you — a one-person product team — for one working system they could realistically pilot: the
rulebooks go in; a trustworthy, prioritized, verified worklist comes out the other end.

Part 3 · Your mission + the hard boundary
Build the system YOU believe serves this clinic, end to end: documents compiled into rules → rules +
calibration measures executed over the messy panel → a product the care team can trust and act on →
operable enough to pilot. You own scope, architecture, stack, and interface. Non-negotiables: whatever
runs against patients is deterministic and inspectable — same input, same output, no model call deciding
a patient’s verdict at runtime (AI compiles; rules execute) · a rule without a verbatim source quote is
structurally impossible · every patient-level output traces to the records and the rule that caused it · setup
in ≤3 commands · no real patient data, ever.

Part 4 · The hard questions (judged against these; how — and how deeply — is up to
you)
On the knowledge side:
1. Your extractor produced a rule. How do you know it’s real — and how do you measure how often extraction
is right? (“It looked correct” is not evidence; decide what is.)
2. What makes a fabricated rule structurally impossible — not just unlikely — in your design?
3. The footnotes and risk-based criteria are where the real medicine lives. How much does your representation
capture, and what did it genuinely fail to capture? (We will look.)
4. A guideline updates (50 → 45). What happens in your system? Who finds the patients that newly qualify?
5. Where is AI genuinely load-bearing in your system, and where did you deliberately refuse it — argued from
failures you actually observed?
On the operation side:
6. A lab value arrives without units; a visit is duplicated. What does your system do — and what does it never
do?
7. The lab feed silently dies for three weeks. What does the clinic see? (The worst answer is “nothing
changes.”)
8. New data arrives nightly. What recomputes, and what does that cost at 10,000 patients — and at 100,000?
9. The care manager marks a patient “handled.” How does the clinic ever know the care actually happened?
What if it didn’t?
10. Can she open your product and know how to spend her 2 hours — in under 2 minutes? With 20
contacts/day across 10,000 patients, who’s first, and can you say why them in one sentence each?
11. Another team builds a mobile app on your system next quarter. What do you hand them?
12. Six months in, what numbers prove to the clinic that your product is working?
Twelve questions will not all get deep answers in the timebox — that is intentional. Choosing which to go deep
on, and saying why, is the test.
